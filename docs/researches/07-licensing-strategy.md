# Open-Source Licensing Strategy for Infrastructure/Database/Streaming Projects

*Research memo to inform a licensing decision for a new Kafka-compatible, object-storage-backed streaming broker written in Rust (conceptually similar to WarpStream/Redpanda/AutoMQ/Bufstream). Compiled 2026-08-12.*

> **How this fits the project:** this is background for a decision we haven't made yet — see [10-open-questions.md](10-open-questions.md) for the open licensing question this doc feeds into. Nothing here should be read as the project's chosen license.

---

## 1. The Open-Core vs. Fully-Open Spectrum, and the Flight from Permissive Licenses

Through roughly 2008–2018, the default license for infrastructure software was permissive (Apache-2.0, MIT, BSD). Starting around 2018, a wave of infrastructure/database vendors moved to "source-available" licenses that are visible and self-hostable but contractually restrict operating the software *as a competing hosted service*. The trigger in almost every case was the same: a hyperscaler (AWS most often, sometimes GCP/Azure/Alibaba/Tencent) launched a managed offering built on the open-source project, capturing the hosting revenue while contributing little/nothing upstream, undermining the commercial open-source company's ability to fund development.

**MongoDB (AGPLv3 → SSPL, 2018).** MongoDB was AGPLv3 but found the AGPL's "network copyleft" was not deterring cloud vendors from offering it as a service. In October 2018 MongoDB introduced the Server Side Public License (SSPL), whose only substantive change from AGPL is an explicit requirement that anyone offering MongoDB "as a service" to third parties must open-source the *entire stack* used to offer that service (management layer, orchestration, monitoring, APIs — not just MongoDB itself). MongoDB was explicit that the trigger was cloud vendors — including Chinese giants Alibaba, Baidu and Tencent — repackaging MongoDB as a managed service. AWS's response was to launch **DocumentDB**, a MongoDB-*wire-protocol-compatible* database built on AWS's own (non-MongoDB) storage engine, deliberately staying compatible only with the pre-SSPL 3.6 API surface and asserting it uses no SSPL code — precisely the "protocol-compatible workaround" playbook a Kafka-compatible project should expect competitors to use regardless of its own license. ([MongoDB press release](https://www.mongodb.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/mongodb-issues-new-server-side-public-license-for-mongodb-community-server); [GeekWire on DocumentDB](https://www.geekwire.com/2019/amazon-web-services-calls-mongodbs-licensing-bluff-documentdb-new-managed-database/); [SSPL, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_Side_Public_License))

**Elastic (Apache-2.0 → SSPL/Elastic License, 2021 → AGPLv3 added back, 2024).** Elastic relicensed Elasticsearch/Kibana away from Apache-2.0 in January 2021 in direct response to AWS's "Open Distro for Elasticsearch," offering a dual SSPL/Elastic License 2.0 choice that barred competing managed-service offerings. AWS responded by forking the last Apache-2.0 version into **OpenSearch**, which went on to build independent Linux Foundation governance and a real multi-vendor contributor base — i.e., the relicensing did not stop the fork, it just moved the fork out of Elastic's control. In August/September 2024, Elastic's CTO announced they were *adding* AGPLv3 as a third license option (alongside SSPL and Elastic License 2.0), explicitly to regain OSI-approved "open source" status and repair community trust, while keeping SSPL/ELv2 available for those preferring it (no forced migration). ([Elastic blog](https://www.elastic.co/blog/elasticsearch-is-open-source-again); [Elastic's Journey from Apache-2.0 to AGPL3](https://pureinsights.com/blog/2024/elastics-journey-from-apache-2-0-to-agpl-3/))

**Redis (BSD-3 → dual RSALv2/SSPLv1, March 2024 → AGPLv3 added, Redis 8, May 2025).** Redis was permissive BSD-3-Clause for its entire life until March 2024, when Redis Ltd. switched Redis 7.4+ to a dual license of the Redis Source Available License v2 (RSALv2) and SSPLv1 — again explicitly citing hyperscalers "reaping the benefits ... without giving back" (per CEO Rowan Trollope's later retrospective). The backlash was severe: Debian and Fedora/Red Hat dropped Redis as non-free, and AWS, Google Cloud, Oracle, and Ericsson jointly forked the last BSD version (7.2) into **Valkey** under Linux Foundation governance — again, exactly the "the community forks around you" outcome. In May 2025, after former creator Salvatore "antirez" Sanfilippo rejoined Redis, the company reversed course again: starting with **Redis 8**, users can choose among RSALv2, SSPLv1, **or the OSI-approved AGPLv3** — restoring Redis's status as genuinely open source while still offering the source-available options for those who want them. As of this writing (Aug 2026) that three-way choice, with AGPLv3 as the recommended/default open-source option, remains current. ([Redis blog: "Redis is now available under the AGPLv3 open source license"](https://redis.io/blog/agplv3/); [Redis blog: dual source-available licensing, 2024](https://redis.io/blog/redis-adopts-dual-source-available-licensing/); [Percona on the license change](https://www.percona.com/blog/the-redis-license-has-changed-what-you-need-to-know/))

**CockroachDB (Apache-2.0 → BSL, 2019 → Enterprise-only source-available, 2024).** Cockroach Labs relicensed core CockroachDB from Apache-2.0 to a permissive variant of the Business Source License in 2019, with founders explicitly writing that their business model assumed "no much-larger platform company" would offer the same product as a service for free — an assumption AWS/Azure/GCP had broken. BSL let anyone use, modify or embed CockroachDB freely except run it as a competing DBaaS, converting to Apache-2.0 automatically three years after each release. In August 2024 Cockroach Labs went further, **discontinuing the free "Core" edition entirely** — the product is now Enterprise-only (source-available, not open source), free for individual devs/students and companies under $10M revenue, paid above that. Rationale per CEO Spencer Kimball: too many mid-size/large companies were using free Core to avoid paying for Enterprise features, not just hyperscalers reselling it. This is a further data point that source-available licensing did not fully solve the monetization problem and the company kept tightening restrictions over time. ([Changelog: "Why we're relicensing CockroachDB"](https://changelog.com/news/why-were-relicensing-cockroachdb-EOaR); [BigDATAwire on 2024 change](https://www.bigdatawire.com/2024/08/19/cockroach-labs-shifts-from-open-core-to-single-enterprise-model/); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/08/19/cockroachdb_abandons_open_core/))

**HashiCorp (MPL-2.0 → BSL, Aug 2023 → Terraform forked as OpenTofu).** HashiCorp switched Terraform (and its other tools) from MPL-2.0 to BSL 1.1 in August 2023, restricting competing commercial offerings. The community response was immediate and organized: the "OpenTF" initiative published a manifesto within days, forked the last MPL-2.0 Terraform codebase within about two weeks, and by September 2023 the Linux Foundation had accepted it as **OpenTofu**, with ~140 companies and 700 individuals publicly pledging support. OpenTofu reached a stable 1.0 release in January 2024 and remains MPL-2.0/community-governed, now a real competitive alternative to Terraform. IBM's 2024 acquisition of HashiCorp did not reverse the BSL decision. This is the clearest example of source-available licensing directly triggering a well-resourced, foundation-backed fork rather than preventing hyperscaler competition. ([OpenTofu: "OpenTofu Announces Fork of Terraform"](https://opentofu.org/blog/opentofu-announces-fork-of-terraform/); [OpenTofu Manifesto](https://opentofu.org/manifesto/); [Spacelift on the license change](https://spacelift.io/blog/terraform-license-change))

**Confluent (mixed model since 2018).** Confluent never relicensed *Apache Kafka* itself — Kafka remains Apache-2.0 under the ASF regardless of Confluent's actions. Instead, Confluent applies the **Confluent Community License** (source-available, not OSI-approved) to some of the value-add pieces it builds on top: ksqlDB, Schema Registry, REST Proxy, and some Kafka Connect connectors, while Kafka Connect and Kafka Streams core stay Apache-2.0. The Community License's only restriction is against offering the covered component "as a service" that competes with Confluent's own SaaS. This is the "open core" pattern applied surgically component-by-component rather than to an entire product, and it's a close structural precedent for a Kafka-compatible broker that might want Apache-2.0 core protocol/engine plus a more restrictive license on premium operational tooling. ([Confluent Community License FAQ](https://www.confluent.io/confluent-community-license-faq/); [Confluent/Kafka FAQ](https://developer.confluent.io/faq/apache-kafka/confluent/))

**Sentry (BSD-3 → BSL, 2019 → Functional Source License, 2023).** Sentry moved from BSD-3 to BSL in 2019 for the same free-riding reasons as the above, using a 36-month conversion-to-Apache-2.0 window, permitting anything except operating a competing "Application Monitoring Service." In November 2023 Sentry went further and introduced its own license, the **Functional Source License (FSL)**, explicitly as an evolution/simplification of BSL: FSL has only one restriction (no competing use) instead of BSL's many configurable parameters, and shortens the automatic conversion window to Apache-2.0/MIT from BSL's usual 4 years down to **2 years**, which Sentry's head of open source argued makes the eventual-openness promise feel less like a token gesture. FSL has since been adopted by a handful of other projects as a "simpler, faster-converting BSL." ([Sentry blog: "Introducing the Functional Source License"](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-the-functional-source-license-freedom-without-free-riding/); [Sentry and the Business Source License](https://www.screamingatmyscreen.com/sentry-and-the-business-source-license/))

**MariaDB — originator of BSL.** The Business Source License itself was authored by Michael "Monty" Widenius and David Axmark (MySQL/MariaDB founders) with Linus Nyman around 2013, first applied to some MariaDB (MaxScale) products, and revised into the now-standard **BSL 1.1** in 2017 after consultation with OSI co-founder Bruce Perens. So the license mechanism that CockroachDB, HashiCorp, Sentry, Couchbase, and Redpanda all later adopted originates from the MySQL/MariaDB lineage, not from any of the later adopters. ([MariaDB BSL FAQ](https://mariadb.com/bsl-faq-mariadb/); [MariaDB BSL 1.1 text](https://mariadb.com/bsl11/))

**The common pattern.** In every case: (a) the trigger was a hyperscaler or well-capitalized competitor monetizing the project as a hosted service with no meaningful upstream contribution; (b) the response was a source-available "non-compete" license, usually with an automatic future conversion to a fully open license; (c) the community/ecosystem reaction ranged from mild grumbling (Confluent's narrow component-level approach) to outright forks with foundation backing (OpenSearch, Valkey, OpenTofu) when the change felt like a bait-and-switch on an already-large, previously-fully-open project; and (d) several vendors (Elastic, Redis) later *walked back* toward fully open licenses once the community damage outweighed the commercial protection, suggesting the strategy has real reputational costs that must be weighed against the free-riding risk, especially for a project trying to *build* a community rather than one that already has one to lose.

## 2. Business Source License (BSL/BUSL) Mechanics

BSL 1.1 (the standard template, published as a trademarked license text usable by anyone) works as follows:

- **Source is published.** Anyone can view, copy, modify, and self-host the code — it is not closed-source or secret.
- **A single field-of-use restriction, licensor-defined.** The license text ships with two fill-in-the-blank fields: an "Additional Use Grant" (what you're allowed to do beyond bare non-production use — e.g., "any use except offering it as a hosted service") and the restricted "excluded purpose." Redpanda's excluded purpose, for example, is offering the software as a "Streaming or Queuing Service" to third parties; Sentry's is a competing "Application Monitoring Service"; CockroachDB's (2019 version) was a competing DBaaS.
- **Automatic conversion date.** On the "Change Date" — a fixed date the licensor sets, commonly **4 years** after each release (MariaDB's original template) — the code automatically re-licenses to the "Change License," which BSL 1.1 requires be GPLv2-or-later or a GPL-compatible open license (in practice, adopters like CockroachDB, Sentry and HashiCorp instead point the Change License at **Apache-2.0**, which is allowed as long as it's specified upfront and is broadly compatible; the "must be GPL or GPL-compatible" clause in the raw MariaDB template is itself something later adopters override in their own copy of the license text — always check the specific project's LICENSE file rather than assume MariaDB's defaults).
- **Not OSI-approved / not "open source."** Because of the field-of-use restriction, BSL fails the Open Source Definition's "no discrimination against fields of endeavor" clause. It is universally described as "source-available," not "open source," and Debian/Fedora will not package it.
- **Violation consequence.** Using the software outside the Additional Use Grant terminates your license rights automatically; continued use requires a separate commercial license from the licensor.
- **Every release re-starts its own clock.** Each version's code has its own Change Date tied to its release date, so a live BSL project is perpetually "rolling" — old versions keep becoming Apache/GPL while the newest version stays restricted, which is what lets the vendor keep the commercial protection indefinitely while still honoring the "eventually open" promise per version. ([MariaDB BSL 1.1 text](https://mariadb.com/bsl11/); [FOSSA on BSL](https://fossa.com/blog/business-source-license-requirements-provisions-history/))

### WarpStream's actual licensing (verified)

WarpStream (acquired by **Confluent** in September 2024 — its Terms of Service page now redirects to `confluent.com/contracts`) is **not** structured as a classic time-limited BSL project with published core source. Based on available evidence:

- The core **Agent binary** is distributed as a compiled binary only, under Confluent/WarpStream's commercial Terms of Service — there is no public repository with the Agent's implementation source. It should be treated as **proprietary/closed-source**, not "source-available" in the BSL sense (no visible source, no future open-license conversion date). Independent analysis of WarpStream by third parties (AutoMQ's comparison posts) likewise characterizes it as "a commercial Kafka-compatible streaming service... not open source," with a BYOC (bring-your-own-cloud) control-plane dependency baked into the product itself.
- **Peripheral tooling is genuinely open source**: WarpStream Labs publishes its Terraform provider (MPL-2.0), Helm charts (MIT), and Docker Compose examples on GitHub (`github.com/warpstreamlabs`) — but these are deployment/integration tooling, not the streaming engine itself.
- Because WarpStream is Kafka-*protocol*-compatible, it needs no proprietary client library at all — any standard Apache-2.0 Kafka client works unmodified, which is presumably why there's nothing to "open source" on the client side.
- Net: WarpStream's licensing strategy is closer to "fully closed-source commercial SaaS/BYOC product with open Kafka-protocol compatibility" than to a BSL-style source-available core. If a prior assumption was "WarpStream open-sourced the Agent under a delayed license," that does not appear to be accurate based on current public evidence — treat any such assumption as unconfirmed. ([AutoMQ: "WarpStream After Confluent Acquisition"](https://www.automq.com/blog/warpstream-after-confluent-acquisition-what-changed); [AutoMQ: "Replace WarpStream with Open-Source-Friendly Kafka"](https://www.automq.com/blog/replace-warpstream-open-source-friendly-kafka); `github.com/warpstreamlabs` public repos)

## 3. Fully Permissive (Apache-2.0 / MIT)

**Pros for this project type:**
- **Matches Apache Kafka's own license.** Positioning as "Kafka-compatible" trades partly on the credibility and ecosystem trust Kafka's Apache-2.0/ASF governance built over a decade. Using the same license category signals "we're playing by the same open-ecosystem rules," which matters when courting Kafka-shop engineers who are often ideologically or organizationally predisposed toward Apache-licensed infra.
- **Zero adoption friction.** No legal review needed at most companies (Apache-2.0 and MIT are on essentially every enterprise's pre-approved license allowlist), so evaluation-to-production time is minimal — critical for a new entrant competing against incumbent Kafka.
- **Maximizes external contribution.** Permissive licenses are the only category with no field-of-use ambiguity for contributors, which matters if you want a genuine outside contributor base and possibly a future foundation home (CNCF, ASF) — both require permissive or copyleft-with-CLA licensing and generally reject BSL/SSPL for incubation.
- **Ecosystem embedding.** Downstream projects (client libraries, Kafka Connect-style connectors, managed platforms, other vendors building on top) can freely embed/redistribute without a commercial negotiation, which grows the addressable ecosystem — this is explicitly how AutoMQ (Apache-2.0) and Apache Kafka itself frame their positioning.

**Cons for this project type:**
- **Maximum hyperscaler free-riding exposure.** A Kafka-compatible, S3-backed broker is *exactly* the profile hyperscalers have targeted before (MongoDB → DocumentDB, Elasticsearch → OpenSearch, Redis → ElastiCache/MemoryDB, Kafka itself → MSK). AWS/GCP/Azure already operate managed Kafka-compatible services; a fully permissive license removes any legal deterrent (though as MongoDB/Redis/Elastic all found, source-available licenses only partially deterred this anyway — see §1).
- **No monetization lever built into the license.** All commercial protection has to come from a separate mechanism (open core, trademark/hosting control, support contracts, managed-service moat, community goodwill) rather than the license itself.

## 4. Copyleft (AGPLv3)

**Mechanism as a deterrent.** AGPLv3 extends GPL's copyleft to network use: if you modify AGPL-licensed software and let others interact with it over a network (i.e., run it as a service), you must offer those users the complete corresponding source of your modified version. This closes the "ASP/SaaS loophole" that ordinary GPL has (GPL only triggers on *distribution*, and running a service isn't distribution). In theory this makes it commercially awkward for a hyperscaler to take an AGPL project, modify it, and sell it as a black-box managed service without releasing their modifications.

**Practical record.** AGPL is real, OSI-approved open source (unlike BSL/SSPL), which is why Elastic and Redis both moved *back to* AGPLv3 in 2024/2025 specifically to regain "actually open source" status while retaining some deterrent value against pure resale. MongoDB started on AGPLv3 before concluding it wasn't a strong enough deterrent and moved to SSPL specifically because AGPL's network-copyleft trigger has known workarounds (a cloud vendor can keep the AGPL-covered component unmodified and build all the differentiating value in a separate, non-AGPL orchestration/control-plane layer, sidestepping the "must release your modifications" trigger). SSPL was MongoDB's response — it explicitly requires releasing the *entire service stack*, not just modifications to the covered code, closing that gap; that stronger requirement is exactly why SSPL fails OSI approval.

**Enterprise adoption friction — the real tradeoff.** Many companies, especially larger enterprises and famously Google, have blanket bans on ingesting **any** AGPL code into internal systems, out of fear that the network-copyleft trigger could force disclosure of proprietary internal source if AGPL code is linked into an internally-run service. Google's stated policy: "aggressively-broad ban on all AGPL software," explicitly prohibiting AGPL code in google3 or any Google product, because "any product or service that depends on AGPL-licensed code ... may be subject to the virality of the AGPL license." This is a well-documented, widespread pattern (not just Google) — many corporate open-source-usage policies flag AGPL (along with SSPL) as "prohibited" or "requires legal approval" categories distinct from ordinary GPL. For an infrastructure component that enterprises would run *inside their own infra* (not as a library linked into a bigger service), the practical risk is lower than for, say, an AGPL logging library — but the categorical/reflexive nature of many corporate AGPL bans means some enterprise prospects will simply refuse to evaluate an AGPL broker at all, regardless of the technical nuance, purely on legal-policy-as-written grounds. ([Google AGPL Policy](https://opensource.google/documentation/reference/using/agpl-policy); [HN discussion: "AGPL license is a non-starter for most companies"](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37903520))

## 5. Kafka-Compatibility-Specific Considerations and Direct Comparables

Apache Kafka itself is Apache-2.0 under ASF governance — fully permissive, no field-of-use restriction. Being protocol-compatible with an Apache-2.0 ecosystem while licensing your own implementation more restrictively is legally unproblematic (protocol/wire-format compatibility is not derivative-work territory), but it does create a **positioning tension**: "Kafka-compatible" pitches often lean on "open, standards-based, no lock-in" messaging, and a restrictive license on the compatible implementation can read as inconsistent with that message to technically sophisticated buyers, even though it's legally fine. It also affects whether the project can realistically become an ASF/CNCF-hosted project later (those foundations require Apache-2.0-family or similarly permissive licensing for incubation) and whether prospective external contributors treat pull requests as "helping build shared infrastructure" vs. "doing free work for a company's proprietary product."

Direct comparables (streaming brokers with S3/object-storage-backed, Kafka-compatible architectures):

| Project | License | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Apache Kafka** | Apache-2.0 | ASF-governed, fully permissive, no field-of-use limits. |
| **Redpanda** | **Redpanda Business Source License (BSL)** for Community Edition; **Redpanda Community License (RCL)** for Enterprise-only features | BSL excluded purpose is specifically offering it as a "Streaming or Queuing Service" to third parties; converts to Apache-2.0 four years after each version's release. Enterprise features gated behind a separate license key under RCL — a classic open-core-on-top-of-BSL structure. ([Redpanda blog](https://www.redpanda.com/blog/bsl-source-available-license); [Redpanda licensing docs](https://docs.redpanda.com/current/get-started/licensing/overview/); [BSL license text in repo](https://github.com/redpanda-data/redpanda/blob/dev/licenses/bsl.md)) |
| **WarpStream** (now Confluent) | Proprietary/closed-source commercial product (BYOC); no public engine source. Peripheral tooling (Terraform provider, Helm charts) is MIT/MPL-2.0. | See §2 above for full detail — this is *not* a time-limited source-available license; it's closed source. |
| **AutoMQ** | **Apache-2.0** (core, confirmed via public GitHub repo license badge) | Positions itself explicitly as the "open-source-friendly" alternative to WarpStream/Redpanda on licensing grounds, forked from/built on the Apache Kafka codebase (so retains Kafka's client/connector/tooling ecosystem) with a diskless S3-backed storage layer added. Uses licensing openness as a direct competitive marketing angle against Redpanda/WarpStream. ([AutoMQ GitHub](https://github.com/automq/automq); [AutoMQ licensing comparison blog](https://www.automq.com/blog/open-source-kafka-licenses-2026-apache-2-bsl-sspl)) |
| **Bufstream** (Buf, now owned by CoreWeave after its 2025 acquisition) | No public source repository found; treated by the market as a **closed-source commercial product** (self-hostable binary, not source-available under a published license). | Buf's own CLI/schema tooling (`buf`) is Apache-2.0, but Bufstream itself does not appear to ship public source; could not verify a specific license name — flag as unconfirmed/likely proprietary pending direct confirmation from Buf/CoreWeave. ([CoreWeave Acquires Bufstream](https://buf.build/blog/coreweave-acquires-bufstream)) |
| **Confluent Platform** | Mixed: Apache Kafka core = Apache-2.0; Schema Registry/ksqlDB/some connectors = Confluent Community License (source-available, non-OSI) | Component-level open-core split rather than whole-product. ([Confluent Community License FAQ](https://www.confluent.io/confluent-community-license-faq/)) |

The market has visibly split into two camps: **Redpanda/WarpStream/Bufstream** all chose restrictive-to-closed licensing on their core engine (protecting against hyperscaler resale and each other), while **AutoMQ** chose full Apache-2.0 and is actively using that as a marketing wedge against the other three. This means a new entrant has a legitimate, already-proven-viable option on *either* side of the spectrum, and "who else did what" is not by itself a tiebreaker — AutoMQ demonstrates permissive licensing is viable to launch with in this exact niche, while Redpanda demonstrates BSL/RCL open-core is viable to build a large commercial company with in this exact niche. See [03-competitive-landscape.md](03-competitive-landscape.md) for the full technical comparison of all these systems.

## 6. CLAs and Dual-Licensing / Open-Core as Alternatives to a Restrictive License

**Contributor License Agreements.** A CLA has contributors grant the project (usually the corporate entity behind it) broad rights to relicense their contributions, beyond what the project's stated open-source license alone would allow. Benefits: gives the maintaining company legal clarity on IP provenance, and critically, the *option to relicense later* — MongoDB's CLA is exactly what let it legally relicense years of community contributions from AGPL to SSPL in 2018 without needing to track down and re-clear every past contributor individually. Costs: CLAs are a well-known adoption deterrent for volunteer contributors (perceived as "signing away your work to a company that might use it against the community's interests" — MongoDB's own history is now the canonical cautionary example cited against CLAs), add contribution friction/overhead, and signal "this is a company's product, not a community's project" to some prospective contributors. A lighter-weight alternative used by many permissively-licensed projects (Linux kernel, most CNCF projects) is the **DCO (Developer Certificate of Origin)** — a per-commit sign-off attesting you have the right to contribute the code under the stated license, with no relicensing rights granted — which preserves the option to move to foundation governance later but forecloses unilateral future relicensing by any single company. If you want the *option* to relicense later (e.g., to add a BSL layer down the road) you need a CLA now; if you want maximum contributor trust and a plausible future foundation donation, DCO + a genuinely permissive license is the more credible signal. ([Contributor License Agreement, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contributor_License_Agreement); [TermsFeed on CLAs](https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/contributor-license-agreements-cla/))

**Dual-licensing / open-core as a non-license-based protection mechanism.** Rather than (or in addition to) restricting the license on the whole codebase, many projects keep the *core* engine permissively licensed (Apache-2.0/MIT) — maximizing adoption, contribution, and Kafka-ecosystem trust — and put commercially differentiated features (multi-region replication, tiered RBAC/audit, managed-control-plane integrations, advanced observability, enterprise SSO, etc.) behind a separate proprietary license or a paid subscription, distributed as closed-source plugins/modules or gated by a license key. This is structurally what Confluent does around Kafka, what Redpanda does with RCL-gated Enterprise features on top of its BSL core, and is the classic "open core" model broadly. Its protection against hyperscaler resale is weaker than a BSL/SSPL restriction on the *core* itself (a hyperscaler can still resell the permissively-licensed core verbatim), but it avoids essentially all of the community-trust and enterprise-adoption costs documented in §1 and §4, and is the option most compatible with eventual CNCF/ASF donation. The realistic mitigations against pure hyperscaler resale under an open-core model are non-license ones: trademark control over the project/product name (a hyperscaler can resell your code but can't call their service by your brand), a managed-service moat that's genuinely hard to replicate operationally, and community/ecosystem velocity that makes the "official" project the default choice regardless of who else hosts a copy. ([Open Core Ventures handbook](https://handbook.opencoreventures.com/open-core-model/); [TermsFeed: Dual Licensing vs Open Core](https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/dual-licensing-vs-open-core/))

## 7. Practical Recommendation Framework (Tradeoffs, Not a Decision)

For a solo/small-team OSS project seeking real community adoption *and* some hedge against pure hyperscaler resale, positioned as a Kafka-compatible alternative, the tradeoffs cluster into roughly four coherent strategies:

| Strategy | What it looks like | Adoption impact | Hyperscaler-resale protection | Precedent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Fully permissive (Apache-2.0)** | Entire engine Apache-2.0 from day one, matching Kafka's own license | Maximum — zero legal friction, foundation-donation-ready, best shot at genuine outside contributors and ecosystem embedding | None from the license itself; must come from brand, velocity, or a later open-core layer | AutoMQ; Apache Kafka itself |
| **B. Open core (Apache-2.0 core + proprietary/paid extensions)** | Broker engine and protocol layer Apache-2.0; enterprise features (multi-tenant control plane, advanced security/observability, hosted offering) closed or license-gated | High — core still frictionless to adopt; some community skepticism about which future features get "held back" | Weak on the core itself, moderate on total offering via feature gap + brand/support moat | Confluent-around-Kafka; Redpanda's RCL-on-BSL variant of this idea |
| **C. BSL/BUSL with a defined excluded purpose (e.g., "cannot offer as a managed streaming/queuing service") and a 2–4 year Apache-2.0 conversion** | Full source published; free for internal/embedded use; commercial hosting requires a paid license; auto-converts to Apache-2.0 per-release after N years | Medium — legible to engineers as "basically open," but triggers legal review at large enterprises and is a hard no for OSI/ASF/CNCF-purity shops; some contributors decline to contribute to non-OSI-licensed projects on principle | Strongest direct deterrent against a hyperscaler selling your code as a managed service unmodified, though history (Elastic→OpenSearch, Redis→Valkey, HashiCorp→OpenTofu) shows determined, well-funded competitors can and will fork the last permissively-licensed version and route around it if the project was ever permissively licensed before | Redpanda; CockroachDB (2019–2024); Sentry; HashiCorp |
| **D. AGPLv3** | Full network copyleft on the whole engine | Medium-low — real OSI-approved open source (good for principle-driven contributors/foundations), but triggers blanket bans at many enterprises regardless of actual risk, which is a direct adoption tax for a broker meant to run inside customer infrastructure | Moderate — deters casual black-box resale of modified code, though (per MongoDB's own experience) determined actors can architect around the network-copyleft trigger by keeping differentiating logic in an unmodified-AGPL-adjacent layer | Elastic (current), Redis (current, as one of three options) |

**Considerations specific to being new/small vs. the incumbents above:**
- Every BSL/SSPL adopter above (MongoDB, Elastic, Redis, CockroachDB, HashiCorp, Sentry) relicensed **away from** an already fully-open, already-adopted project under existing-community pressure — none of them *launched* as source-available and then had to defend that choice to a pre-existing community. Starting BSL/restrictive from day one (as Redpanda did) avoids the "bait and switch" reputational damage those retroactive changes caused, but also means you never get the initial adoption curve that comes from launching as unambiguously "real" open source — a bigger relative cost for a small team that most needs community-driven adoption momentum (bug reports, protocol edge-case testing, connector/tooling contributions) to compete credibly against Kafka's decade of ecosystem maturity.
- A hyperscaler cannot realistically build a competing managed offering around a project with negligible adoption in the first place — the free-riding risk this whole licensing debate is about only *becomes* real once the project has real traction. That argues for optimizing for adoption velocity first (Strategy A or B) and treating a later move to a BSL-style excluded-purpose clause (Strategy C), applied prospectively to new releases only, as a reversible decision available once there's something worth protecting — mirroring what Cockroach Labs, HashiCorp, and Sentry all actually did (they were years into an already-permissive project before adding restrictions), rather than what MongoDB/Redis's retroactive whiplash episodes show is costly to reverse-engineer trust from after the fact.
- If a CLA is desired to preserve future relicensing optionality (i.e., to keep Strategy C available as a later pivot from Strategy A/B), get it in place from the very first external contribution — retrofitting a CLA after an active contributor base exists is far more contentious (see MongoDB precedent in §6) than requiring it from commit #1 when there's no existing community to alienate.
